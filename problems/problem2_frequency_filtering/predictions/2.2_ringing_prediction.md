# Pre-implementation prediction — §2.2 Ringing

**Date: 2026-10-19** (written and committed before running any code for §2.2)

## Setup

The step-edge image `s(x,y)` is a bright `B=200` square of side 256 centred on an
`A=50` background, on a 512x512 grid, filtered per the mandated pipeline: zero-pad to
`P = 2·512 = 1024`, multiply by a centred radial filter `H(u,v)`, invert, crop.

Because the image varies only along `x` near the middle of an edge (far from corners),
its 2-D Fourier transform is confined to the line `v=0`
(`F(u,v) = F_1D(u) · δ(v)` for a signal that is constant in `y`), so only the
1-D slice `H(u,0)` of the radially symmetric `H` affects the profile along a row through
the middle of the square.

## Ideal low-pass filter — closed-form prediction

Model the edge locally as a continuous 1-D unit step of height `B-A` at `x=0`, and the
ideal filter as a continuous bandlimiting operator with cutoff frequency `W` (in
cycles/pixel). This is the classical derivation (Gibbs phenomenon):

```
g(x) = (A+B)/2 + (B-A)/π · Si(2πWx),     Si(z) = ∫₀^z sin(t)/t dt
```

**Overshoot.** `g(x)` first peaks where `d/dx Si(2πWx) = 0`, i.e. `sin(2πWx)=0` at the
first nonzero point `2πWx = π`. There, `Si(π) ≈ 1.8519`. The overshoot above `B` is

```
g_peak - B = (B-A)/π · (Si(π) - π/2) = (B-A) · 0.0895...
```

so the predicted **overshoot is ≈ 8.95% of the jump height `B-A`**, and — this is the
notable part — it is **independent of the cutoff `W` (equivalently of `D0`)**. This is
the textbook Gibbs-phenomenon result: sharper cutoffs ring at higher spatial frequency
but not with larger amplitude.

**First-peak distance.** The peak occurs at `2πWx_peak = π`, i.e.

```
x_peak = 1/(2W) pixels
```

Translating to the discrete grid: the padded grid has `P=1024` samples, and filter
cutoff index `D0` corresponds to continuous frequency `W = D0/P` cycles/pixel (DFT bin
spacing is `1/P` cycles/pixel). So

```
x_peak = P / (2·D0) pixels
```

**Predictions to check** for `D0 ∈ {10, 20, 40, 80}` (all computed with `P=1024`):

| D0 | predicted overshoot | predicted x_peak (px) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | ≈8.95% | 51.2 |
| 20 | ≈8.95% | 25.6 |
| 40 | ≈8.95% | 12.8 |
| 80 | ≈8.95% | 6.4 |

I expect the measured overshoot to be *close to* 8.95% for all four, but with growing
deviation at small `D0` (10, 20), because: (1) the continuous-frequency model assumes a
cutoff sharply defined in continuous frequency, while the DFT only samples `D0` at
discrete radii, so a small `D0` is represented by very few frequency samples and the
"circular" 2-D cutoff (a disk, not a 1-D band) departs more from the ideal 1-D band
model when the disk radius is small relative to the grid; (2) the step's finite size
(side 256) means the two edges of the square are close enough together at small `D0`
(wide spatial ringing extent `~x_peak`) that their ringing patterns could overlap and
distort each other, which I'd expect to matter most exactly at the smallest `D0` where
`x_peak` is largest (51.2 px, ~20% of the half-width of the square).

I also expect the one-dimensional argument to **break down near a corner** of the
square: a corner is not a 1-D step but a 2-D discontinuity with contributions from two
perpendicular edges, so the simple `v=0`-slice argument (which assumed the signal is
constant in `y`) no longer applies, and I'd expect a different, probably more complex,
ringing pattern there (overshoot magnitude and shape not predicted by the formula
above).

## Butterworth low-pass filter

Predict that overshoot grows with order `n`: at `n=1`, the Butterworth spatial kernel
is known to stay close to non-negative (a smooth, monotonic low-pass with gentle
roll-off), so I predict **little to no overshoot** at `n=1`; as `n` increases toward
the ideal-filter limit (`n→∞` recovers the ideal filter's hard cutoff), I predict
overshoot should **increase monotonically with `n`**, approaching the ~8.95%
ideal-filter limit from below as `n` grows large (e.g. `n=20` should look close to the
ideal-filter case).

## Gaussian low-pass filter

The Gaussian's spatial-domain kernel is itself a Gaussian, which is strictly
**non-negative everywhere and integrates (sums, discretely) to 1** (it's `H(0,0)=1` at
DC by construction, i.e. total gain at zero frequency is preserved, and a Gaussian is
positive everywhere). A convolution with a non-negative, sum-to-one kernel is a
weighted average of the input — and a weighted average of values that never exceed `B`
cannot itself exceed `B`. So I predict **exactly zero overshoot** for the Gaussian
filter at any `D0`, i.e. a purely monotonic, smooth transition from `A` to `B` with no
ringing whatsoever.

## What I'll check against these predictions

- Measured overshoot vs. the ~8.95% ideal-filter prediction, across all four D0 values,
  explicitly reconciling the discrete-grid deviations discussed above.
- Measured first-peak pixel distance vs. `P/(2D0)`.
- Butterworth overshoot-vs-n trend: monotonically increasing, `n=1` near-zero, high n
  approaching the ideal limit.
- Gaussian overshoot: should measure as ≈0% (up to floating-point noise) at every D0.
